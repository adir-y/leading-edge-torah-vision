I. The Core Claim

This document makes one specific claim: Jewish text and law contain a theory of people, organizations, and leadership that is genuinely different from the frameworks that dominate modern management thinking.

Modern management thinking mostly rests on an Enlightenment idea of the person: an independent individual, guided by reason, pursuing their own interests, protected by a framework of rights. Jewish tradition starts somewhere else: a person bound by obligation, embedded in relationship, pursuing purpose within a covenant, a mutual, unconditional commitment. That creates a new starting point for Jewish nonprofits. We're all a collective, no matter our backgrounds, grounded in a shared obligation and covenantal relationship.

Followed to its logical end, this tradition would say the office, the hiring process, and the performance review are where some of the most consequential work happens, building a workplace where something sacred can actually live. This shifts those elements of talent, culture, and leadership from administrative to sacred.

II. Foundational Ideas

Six main ideas that are the bedrock from which our educational approach is formed.

1. People Contain Multitudes

Jewish tradition describes two souls in permanent tension inside every person: the nefesh ha'elokit (the Divine-facing soul), which wants meaning, and the nefesh ha'behemit (the animal soul), which wants comfort, status, and safety. In plain terms: everyone genuinely wants to matter, and everyone also resists change that threatens their comfort, often about the very same thing, at the very same time. Leading Edge's work is to channel ambition and the desire for recognition toward the mission, not to pretend either soul isn't in the room.

Source: Tanya, Likkutei Amarim, chs. 1–2 (Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi).

2. Leadership as Burden, Rather Than Achievement

Torah treats leadership as a burden instead of a prize. The Midrash, the body of ancient rabbinic commentary that expands on the Torah's stories, describes God placing burdens on Israel's elders in place of greatness. The real danger of a leadership role is ego. Bittul (self-transparency to purpose — staying out of your own way) has to be practiced daily, or the role's default drift is toward serving the leader instead of the mission.

Source: Numbers 11:17, interpreted by Rabbi Sacks

“I will come down and speak with you there, and I will draw upon the spirit that is on you and put it upon them; they shall share the burden of the people with you, and you shall not bear it alone.”

“Judaism teaches that leadership should be shared. Every person matters. Everyone should be educated, responsible, and part of the team. Good leaders don't try to do everything or gain all the power. They guide, empower, and inspire others to step up. That's why God said to Moshe, ‘I will take some of the spirit that is on you and place it upon them.’ Great leaders share their spirit. They don't treat people like children, but help them become adults who can lead too. People become what their leader gives them the space to become. When that space is large, they grow into greatness.”

3. Character Before Competence, Faithfulness Before Outcomes

Every appointment story in the tradition puts character ahead of skill; hiring, promotion, and succession should follow the same order. Evaluation should hold faithfulness (ne'emanut) alongside results. Moses is called God's faithful servant, not God's successful servant. When the two are in tension, faithfulness wins. That doesn't mean results don't count: the two are meant to be read together.

Source: Numbers 12:7 — “Not so with My servant Moses; he is trusted (ne'eman) throughout My household.”

4. Design for Weakness, Ritualize the Response

Torah designs for the worst moment of the best person, not an average day. That means building structural fences that hold even when someone's judgment fails, and mandating practice instead of waiting for buy-in first; “the heart follows the hands” is the operating principle. Character is a habit built by repetition rather than a belief that produces behavior on its own. In a nonprofit, that means being proactive about the structures we build rather than assuming good intentions will carry the day.

Source: Exodus 24:7 (na'aseh v'nishma — “we will do and we will hear”).

Note: this idea functions as a design instruction for how to build programming, not solely as content to teach directly.

5. Covenant, Accountability, and Disagreement

Most organizational relationships are contracts: conditional and transactional. Torah's frame is covenant (brit), unconditional at the foundation, about identity rather than exchange. Inside a covenant, accountability takes the form of tokhehah: private, calibrated feedback aimed at transformation. Withholding honest feedback to spare someone's feelings, while understandable, can lead to toxic work environments. Disagreement has its own name and standard too: mahloket l'shem shamayim, disagreement for the sake of heaven. The Sanhedrin, the tradition's high court, required its most junior judges to speak first, and treated a unanimous guilty verdict as grounds for suspicion, in that too much agreement was itself a warning sign.

Source: Pirkei Avot 5:17 (mahloket l'shem shamayim); Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:2 (junior judges speak first).

6. Time, Teshuvah, and Community as Organism

Different periods of the calendar (Elul, the Omer, and other holiday-adjacent mechanisms) carry different developmental work. Teshuvah, usually translated as repentance or return, asks for more than continuous improvement. It requires naming the failure out loud, and a real test: facing the same situation again and choosing differently. And community, in this tradition, is closer to a single living organism. Damage to trust between two people injures something the whole group holds in common, which is also why a manager's deepest obligation is to notice what someone can't yet see in themselves.

Source: Rambam, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Teshuvah 2:1.

III. Five Structural Patterns

These five tensions recur across all six ideas above. They're worth naming on their own, as recognition of the spectrum of these is part and parcel of the Torah that we teach.

  • Realism vs. aspiration: design for how people actually behave, not how they should behave.
  • Paradox held, not resolved: Torah keeps contradictions at full strength instead of smoothing them into a single synthesis.
  • Long view vs. this year's plan: three thousand years of observation, next to a three-to-five-year strategic horizon.
  • Integration vs. compartmentalization: the same character makes a good spouse and a good leader — work and life aren't separate moral zones.
  • Obligation vs. rights: the operating question is “what do I owe,” not “what am I entitled to.”

IV. From Idea to Practice — The Four Domains

This draft now follows the same four categories as the Standards, in the same order, and speaks directly to the specific practices, the named Standards, inside each one, rather than staying at the level of general themes. Three examples carry most of the weight across all four: the Sanhedrin–Rabbinical Court (shared judgment), tokhehah (feedback), and the Mishkan–portable sanctuary (practice before belief). Where a domain doesn't need a new source, it borrows one of these three rather than introducing a fresh one.

Talent Acquisition

Standards: Talent Strategy, Job Postings, Sourcing Candidates, Screening and Selection, Compensation and Benefits, Retention.

Character is the primary hiring criterion, inside Screening and Selection: reference checks should probe how someone behaves under pressure rather than just what they delivered. Inside Job Postings and Talent Strategy, job design should state a role's boundaries as clearly as its requirements. Bounded authority is what keeps a leadership role from drifting toward ego, the same danger named in Foundational Idea 2. And across Compensation and Benefits and the offer process generally, the heavier obligation falls on the employer: ambiguity should favor the candidate, and hiring someone into a known dysfunction without disclosing it is treated as a real breach of the aspirational covenant we are trying to create.

Growth and Development

Standards: Onboarding, Individual Development, Manager Training, Promotions, Succession Planning, Offboarding and Exits.

Every practice in this category rests on one claim: growth is something a person is owed, independent of how useful they are to the mission this quarter. Inside Onboarding and Manager Training, that argues for chavruta, paired, mutual learning over one-directional instruction; a lot of what a good manager knows is passed on by proximity to one and less so by a training module.

Inside Individual Development, Promotions, and Succession Planning, the Mussar tradition, a centuries-old Jewish discipline of character formation, has a direct answer to the gap between knowing and doing: work on one trait at a time. In short daily sessions, paired with practice and, where realistic, a nightly self-check (heshbon ha'nefesh), an organization can foster an environment of self-growth as part of the organizational ethos. Development plans can also reflect that notion of a repeated practice instead of a single conversation. Offboarding and Exits deserve the same honesty tokhehah–feedback asks for elsewhere, a real, calibrated conversation about what didn't work.

Performance and Accountability

Standards: Role Definition, Feedback, Staff Voice, Performance Management, Values Accountability.

Role Definition and Values Accountability should hold two measures side by side: din–honest assessment (what someone did) and heshbon–accounting (what they could have done, given who they are and what they had). Two people can hit the same number and deserve two different conversations.

Feedback is where tokhehah applies most directly: obligatory more so than optional, private, specific, and aimed at the other person's growth. Staff Voice and Performance Management both benefit from the Sanhedrin's model of shared judgment: junior voices go first, and a single evaluator's read on a consequential outcome shouldn't be the whole story, any more than a unanimous verdict should be taken at face value without a second look. And a mistake that's been fully processed, named clearly, tested by facing the same situation again, should count as evidence of a person's capacity to grow, which is what teshuvah–return to one's best self actually measures.

Organizational Culture

Standards: Belonging, Jewish Thought and Practice, Mission and Values, Internal Communications, Decision Making, Constructive Dialogue, Celebration and Rituals, Risk Taking and Innovation.

Belonging and Mission and Values both rest on the claim in Foundational Idea 6: community is more akin to a living organism than a group of individuals. Belonging work follows the same claim about what damages or strengthens something the whole group holds in common. Celebration and Rituals, and Risk Taking and Innovation, follow the narratives around the Mishkan, the portable sanctuary that traveled with the Israelites in the desert. It was built through precise, specified practice, and belief followed the doing (na'aseh v'nishma) rather than the other way around.

Practicing a specific, repeated ritual turns a value from abstract into something real, which is also the logic behind the daily blessings (berakhot) in Jewish life. That practice transforms dozens of small interruptions of attention built into an ordinary day into a sustainable culture. Internal Communications and Constructive Dialogue are served by boundaries and limits. The laws of lashon hara–ethics of speech, hold that true speech can still cause real damage. Disagreement has its own standard, mahloket l'shem shamayim, disagreement for the sake of betterment, paired with the Sanhedrin's structure, junior voices first, easy consensus treated as a warning sign, as the model for what that looks like in practice. Jewish Thought and Practice, as a named Standard in its own right, is arguably what this entire document is trying to make concrete, which is exactly the open question at the end of this draft.

Board Standards of Excellence

Board/Professional Partnership

Standards: Professional Talent and Workplace, Board Chair Partnership, Fundraising and Sustainability, CEO Oversight and Accountability, CEO Succession Planning.

Board Chair Partnership and CEO Oversight and Accountability both rest on Foundational Idea 5: covenant, not contract. A board-CEO relationship built on brit, unconditional at the foundation, makes room for tokhehah, private and calibrated feedback aimed at the CEO's growth rather than a scorecard exercise. CEO Succession Planning deserves the same honesty tokhehah asks for elsewhere: a real conversation about what a transition requires. And Professional Talent and Workplace, together with Fundraising and Sustainability, follow the same rule that governs hiring in Foundational Idea 3: the heavier obligation falls on the board, and ambiguity about what the organization can and can't sustain should favor the staff and the mission.

Performance and Accountability

Standards: Roles and Responsibilities, Gatherings, Board Operations, Engagement and Assessment, Goal Setting.

Roles and Responsibilities and Goal Setting should hold two measures side by side: din (what the board accomplished) and heshbon (what it could have accomplished, given who sits on it and what it had to work with). Gatherings and Board Operations benefit directly from the Sanhedrin's model of shared judgment: junior voices go first, and a single influential trustee's read on a consequential decision shouldn't be the whole story, any more than a unanimous vote should be taken at face value without a second look. And Engagement and Assessment should measure teshuvah as much as output, whether a board that missed something last year has named the failure and actually chosen differently this year rather than solely whether this year's numbers look better.

Board Talent

Standards: Cultivating Talent, Life Cycles, Recruitment and Vetting, Onboarding, Board Succession Planning.

Character is the primary hiring criterion, inside Recruitment and Vetting: reference checks should probe how a prospective board member behaves under pressure and disagreement, not just what they've accomplished or who they know. Life Cycles and Onboarding follow Foundational Idea 4: design for the worst moment of the best person by building a structured path (orientation, committee placement, a real onboarding plan) rather than trusting good intentions to carry a new member. And Board Succession Planning applies the Sanhedrin's own criteria for judges directly: wisdom, humility, and a clear understanding of how power gets abused are the traits Cultivating Talent should be screening for.

Board Culture

Standards: Purpose and Values, Jewish Thought and Practice, Belonging, Communication and Transparency, Growth Opportunities, Constructive Dialogue and Disagreement, Risk Taking and Innovation.

Purpose and Values and Belonging both rest on the claim in Foundational Idea 6: a board is closer to one living organism than a collection of individuals, so how members treat each other in the room is itself the culture. Constructive Dialogue and Disagreement follows machloket l'shem shamayim, disagreement for the sake of heaven, with the Sanhedrin's own structure as the model: junior trustees speak first, and quick, easy consensus is treated as a warning sign rather than a sign of health.

Communication and Transparency needs the same real limit as lashon hara names: true information shared at the wrong time or in the wrong way can still cause real damage to trust. And Growth Opportunities and Risk Taking and Innovation follow the Mishkan: a board builds a genuine learning culture through precise, repeated practice, study and debriefs after challenging decisions rather than ad-hoc curiosity. Jewish Thought and Practice, named here as it is inside Organizational Culture, is what should be shaping how a board actually behaves in the room using Jewish concepts named here and others brought up organically to ground real life conversations. A d'var torah is a nice table setter but shouldn't be the only intervention.

The Standards name specific ways boards support each domain: resourcing talent strategy, modeling a growth mindset, holding leadership accountable to clear goals, and treating culture as a strategic priority rather than an afterthought. The Sanhedrin's own criteria for judges is a useful lens across all four: wisdom, humility, and a clear understanding of how power gets abused. Board culture should be generative and should be wary about being overly transactional in its dealings, as it tends to produce a transactional organization underneath it; a board that embodies kedushah, a sense of the sacred, creates the conditions for the whole organization to do the same.

Talent Acquisition

Standards: Talent Strategy, Job Postings, Sourcing Candidates, Screening and Selection, Compensation and Benefits, Retention.

Hire for character as much as skill, name a role's boundaries as clearly as its requirements, and give candidates the benefit of the doubt on ambiguity — the heavier obligation falls on the employer.

Try this: In your next reference check, ask one question about what the candidate delivered, and two about how they behaved when something went wrong.

Growth and Development

Standards: Onboarding, Individual Development, Manager Training, Promotions, Succession Planning, Offboarding and Exits.

Growth is something a person is owed, not a reward for being useful — pair new hires with a peer for mutual learning (chavruta), and build skills one trait at a time through short, repeated practice (Mussar) rather than one-time trainings.

Try this: Pair every new hire with someone outside their reporting line in month one, and pick one trait for a team to practice together for a month.

Performance and Accountability

Standards: Role Definition, Feedback, Staff Voice, Performance Management, Values Accountability.

Judge both what someone did and what they could have done given their circumstances (din/heshbon); give feedback privately, specifically, and soon (tokhehah); let junior voices speak first; and treat real, demonstrated change as evidence of growth (teshuvah).

Try this: In your next review, ask two questions before you write the rating — what happened, and what could this person have done given what they actually had?

Organizational Culture

Standards: Belonging, Jewish Thought and Practice, Mission and Values, Internal Communications, Decision Making, Constructive Dialogue, Celebration and Rituals, Risk Taking and Innovation.

Treat the organization as one connected system, not a collection of individuals — small, repeated rituals build culture faster than stated values, honest speech still has real limits (lashon hara), and disagreement done well (mahloket l'shem shamayim) is a sign of health, not dysfunction.

Try this: Pick one small, repeated ritual — an opening question at every staff meeting, a specific way you mark someone's first week — and hold it consistently for a full quarter before judging whether it's working.

Board Standards of Excellence

Board/Professional Partnership

Standards: Professional Talent and Workplace, Board Chair Partnership, Fundraising and Sustainability, CEO Oversight and Accountability, CEO Succession Planning.

The board-CEO relationship is a covenant, not a scorecard — real feedback and honest conversation about what's sustainable matter more than the numbers alone, and the heavier obligation falls on the board.

Try this: Before the board's next CEO evaluation, spend five minutes on what a good-faith, forward-looking conversation about growth would sound like.

Performance and Accountability

Standards: Roles and Responsibilities, Gatherings, Board Operations, Engagement and Assessment, Goal Setting.

Judge the board the way Foundational Idea 3 asks you to judge staff — by what it accomplished and what it could have accomplished given what it had to work with — and let junior voices speak first.

Try this: At your next board meeting, ask the newest or most junior trustee to weigh in on a real decision before anyone else does.

Board Talent

Standards: Cultivating Talent, Life Cycles, Recruitment and Vetting, Onboarding, Board Succession Planning.

Recruit and onboard board members the way you'd hire staff — for character under pressure, with a real structured path, not just good intentions.

Try this: Build an actual 90-day onboarding plan for new board members, not just an orientation packet.

Board Culture

Standards: Purpose and Values, Jewish Thought and Practice, Belonging, Communication and Transparency, Growth Opportunities, Constructive Dialogue and Disagreement, Risk Taking and Innovation.

How board members treat each other in the room is the culture — quick, easy consensus is a warning sign, not a win, and a board that embodies kedushah creates the conditions for the whole organization to do the same.

Try this: Build five minutes of structured disagreement, or a designated devil's-advocate role, into any board vote that actually matters.

V. How Change Actually Happens

Internal change moves far slower than external change. Leaving Egypt physically took one night; the psychological work of actually leaving took forty years in the wilderness. Changing an org chart is different work than changing the people inside it.

Change happens through daily practice (ritual and minhag). Setbacks are to be expected as part of the ebbing and flowing of such culture. In rabbinic parlance, we call that Ratzo v'shov, advance and retreat; it's the normal shape of change. This is the same logic behind the Mishkan: practice first, belief follows (na'aseh v'nishma).

Different moments call for different leaders, and sometimes the form itself has to change. When the Temple fell, Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai chose to build a new center of learning at Yavneh rather than wait for the old structure to return. The most courageous version of change can be letting a structure end so the mission underneath it survives.

Source: Talmud Bavli, Gittin 56a–b (Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai and Yavneh).

VI. Design Philosophy

Our design work should center on nurturing the why. We start from the idea that an organization should love the people in it and respect the yetzer, the internal drive, good and bad, that lives in every person. Build a culture of practice, and trust the heart to follow the hands rather than the other way around.

Just as Jewish law builds “fences” to protect what matters most, we should build fences for people's weakest moment, not just an average day. Treat disagreement as how truth actually emerges. And build for the generations who inherit what gets built, without losing sight of what's due this quarter.

In practice, that design philosophy keeps coming back to the same three pictures: the Sanhedrin, for what shared judgment looks like; tokhehah, for what honest feedback looks like; and the Mishkan, for how practice builds belief rather than the reverse. The aspiration underneath all of it is the same: building organizations where something sacred can actually dwell.

Key Terms

An expanded glossary. Every term below is also translated in plain English the first time it's used above — this is here for quick reference, not as the only place these ideas are explained. Each entry also carries a source and a modeled line — what it might sound like in an actual room.

Bittul

Self-transparency to purpose; the discipline of not letting ego attach to a leadership role.

Source: not pinned to a single text — a live thread across Hasidic thought broadly.

In practice: Before this meeting, name one thing you want people to remember happened — then ask if that's about you or about the work.

Brit (Covenant)

An unconditional, identity-level commitment — the frame Torah uses for organizational relationships, instead of a transactional contract.

Source: Exodus 19:5 (the Sinai covenant language).

In practice: Ask onboarding cohorts: what would you do here even if no one were checking?

Chavruta

Paired, mutual learning — the traditional way Jewish text is studied, and a model for how leadership knowledge transfers best.

Source: Ta'anit 23a (“chavruta o mituta”).

In practice: Pair every new hire with someone outside their reporting line in month one — going beyond managing them, just to think with them.

Cheshbon hanefesh

A structured nightly self-examination practice.

Source: Chovot HaLevavot, Eighth Treatise (Bachya ibn Pakuda).

In practice: Before a hard conversation, write one sentence: what did I actually do about this, not what did I intend to do.

Din / Cheshbon

Two measures held together in evaluation: din is what someone did (the outcome); cheshbon is what they could have done, given who they are and what they had.

Source: a Mussar-era conceptual pairing more than a single cited text.

In practice: In a review, ask two questions instead of one: what happened, and what could this person have done given what they actually had.

Kedushah

Sanctity — the sense of the sacred a board or organization can embody or fail to embody.

Source: Leviticus 19:2 (“kedoshim tihyu” — “you shall be holy”).

In practice: Ask a board before a vote: does this feel like we're being trusted with something, or like we're managing a transaction?

Lashon hara

The Jewish laws around speech holding that true statements can still cause real, unjustified damage.

Source: rooted in Talmudic sources (e.g. Arachin 15b); codified by later legal sources.

In practice: Before sending that email, ask: is this true, necessary, and is now the right time.

Machloket l'shem shamayim

Disagreement “for the sake of heaven” — disciplined, good-faith argument in pursuit of truth, not point-scoring.

Source: Pirkei Avot 5:17.

In practice: When two people disagree in a meeting, ask the room: is this about the mission, or about who's right?

Mussar

A centuries-old Jewish practice of character development, built on daily, narrow, repeated work rather than general resolve.

Source: Rabbi Yisrael Salanter's 19th-century Mussar movement.

In practice: Pick one trait for the month, not five, and check in on just that one at each 1:1.

Ne'emanut

Faithfulness — the standard the tradition applies to Moses, held alongside (not instead of) results.

Source: Numbers 12:7.

In practice: In a reference check, ask less about what they delivered and more about a time they kept a commitment when it got expensive to.

Ratzo v'shov

“Running and returning” — advance and retreat as the normal rhythm of change, not a sign it's failing.

Source: Ezekiel 1:14, adapted in later Hasidic thought as a spiritual rhythm — the organizational-change reading here is our own extension.

In practice: Tell a team mid-project: the pullback you're feeling isn't failure, it's the normal rhythm of real change.

Teshuvah

Return/repair — more demanding than “continuous improvement” because it requires naming the failure and proving, when the same moment comes again, that you'd choose differently.

Source: Rambam, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Teshuvah 2:1.

In practice: Ask someone after a misstep: if the exact same moment happened again tomorrow, what would you do differently — and how would we know?

Tokhehah

Rebuke as an act of love; Torah's structured feedback ethic — obligatory, private, and aimed at transformation.

Source: Leviticus 19:17 (“you shall surely rebuke your neighbor”).

In practice: Give the feedback in private, specifically, and soon.

Yetzer

The internal drive or inclination in every person — not inherently good or bad, but something to be channeled rather than suppressed or indulged.

Source: Genesis 8:21, developed extensively in rabbinic literature (e.g. Berachot 61a).

In practice: Name the ambition in the room instead of pretending it isn't there, then point it at the mission.
