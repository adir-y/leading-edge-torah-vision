1. Role Mandate, In Brief

The VP of Jewish Learning exists to make sure the “Jewish” in Leading Edge's work is not an adjunct to strategy but a driving force behind it, expressed in programs, conferences, field resources, research, team learning, and partnerships. The role sits at the intersection of strategy, implementation, and field leadership, reporting to the Chief Impact Officer (Aviva).

Three bodies of work anchor the position:

  • Field Strategy & Jewish Integration: partnering with the Exec Team to embed Jewish framing into Leading Edge's strategy, and creating the “Leading Edge Torah”: a distinctive Jewish framing that grounds the org's values and approach to leadership and organizational excellence.
  • Field Implementation: infusing the Talent & Culture Standards and Board Standards with deeper Jewish grounding, and designing programs, resources, and evaluation mechanisms that bring Jewish thought into real-world talent, organizational, and governance practice.
  • Thought Leadership & Public Engagement: serving as a public voice on Jewish thought in leadership and organizational excellence, and building the ecosystem of partners committed to this work.

2. Where Things Stand

Leading Edge enters this work from real strength. The 2025–2029 Strategy already names the ambition clearly: the Talent & Culture Standards are described as having “DEIB principles and Jewish values woven throughout,” and the Board has already engaged directly on “the Jewish” as a topic. The organization has invested in exploratory work with Yehudi Meshchaninov, secured a two-year Maimonides Fund partnership explicitly to build out this role and the broader Jewish work, and produced strong proof points: use of the concept of bein adam l'makom, chaveiro, atzmo in OI and embedded Jewish wisdom, thought, and practice in the BLA.

At the same time, early 1:1s across the team surfaced a consistent, candid theme: there is a good deal of talk about “the Jewish” at Leading Edge, but it is not yet consistently reflected in the work itself. A few patterns are worth naming directly:

  • Explicit vs. implicit. Jewish framing shows up in internal rituals (retreats, opening kavanot) more consistently than in external-facing programs and materials. Several team members noted that Jewish content has been diminished as messaging has broadened to appeal to wider audiences — a real tension between reach and distinctiveness.
  • “Window dressing” vs. driving force. The scoping conversation with Yehudi surfaced a live and unresolved question: should Jewish content be a layer applied to already-designed programs and standards, or should Jewish thought actively shape what those programs and standards are in the first place? This is the central design choice for the role.
  • The Standards today. Reviewing the Talent & Culture Standards of Excellence directly: Jewish values are named as an aspiration in one place (the Talent Strategy standard, which calls for organizations to “intentionally embed Jewish values, DEIB, and technology”) but are largely absent from the other eleven standards and from the Board Standards materials.
  • The Learning Management Resources tells a similar story — only 7.5% of the 40 curated resources originate from Jewish sources, a gap the Phase 1 report itself flags for Phase 2.
  • Appetite exists. Program staff (P2C programming, Board Leadership Accelerator, “The Well,” Onboarding Intensive) independently raised “can this have Jewish content?” without being prompted. There is real, organic pull toward this work, not just top-down mandate.
  • The audience is broader than it looks. Leading Edge also serves non-Jewish professionals working inside Jewish organizations. The JPro affinity-group survey found strong interest in more (and more experiential) Jewish learning among this group, alongside a clear ask: don't assume prior knowledge, and don't make it mandatory beyond what the job requires. Any Jewish learning strategy needs to work for both audiences.
  • Leading Edge treats Jewish wisdom, thought, and practice as two separate registers, a decorative quote and a curricular module, rather than as the operating logic of the governance content itself. The result is a genuinely well-crafted onboarding product, with real moments of textual integration, but the Jewish content mostly sits alongside the how-to rather than inside it. That distinction matters both for how materials land with lay leaders who are skeptical of religious framing in ostensibly secular governance work, and for how portable this material is to the non-religious leadership frameworks we're building.

3. Emerging Vision (Draft)

A first pass at a vision statement:

Leading Edge models and helps the Jewish nonprofit sector practice the idea that Jewish thought, values, and tradition are a foundational piece of organizational excellence. When a Jewish organization gets talent, culture, leadership, or governance right, it should be visibly, substantively Jewish in how it got there.

Concretely, this means building the “Leading Edge Torah” — a working body of Jewish framing, texts, and practice that:

  • Gives Leading Edge staff a shared, confident language for why and how Jewish tradition informs this work internally.
  • Gives the field (through the Standards, programs, and Assessment tool) concrete, usable ways to bring Jewish values into talent and governance practice instead of a model that references Jewish ethics in the abstract.
  • Is authentic and accessible across a wide range of Jewish identity, observance, and knowledge, including the many non-Jewish professionals working inside these organizations.
  • Gives Leading Edge a distinct and unique public voice.

4. Strategic Pillars

Pillar 1 — Leading Edge Torah (Field Strategy & Internal Alignment)

Develop and steward the Jewish framing that grounds Leading Edge's own values, language, and approach, in partnership with the Executive Team, informed by past consultants' work, and stewarded through the Jewish Advisory Council structure (building on the Standards Advisory Council model).

This starts at home. Leading Edge can't credibly ask the field to embed Jewish values and practice into culture and governance if staff learning isn't treated as a first-order priority. Concretely, this means building regular, low-lift Jewish learning into how the LE team already works: team meetings, Slack message culture, retreats, onboarding, and shared study of source texts tied to the Standards rather than a stand-alone training people opt into.

This will also be reinforced via in-person staff training at our upcoming staff retreat, along with regularly scheduled virtual sessions on implementing the Jewish learning vision and strategy.

Success looks like staff using shared Jewish language and framing in day-to-day work without being prompted to.

Pillar 2 — Standards Infusion (Field Implementation)

Move Jewish grounding from one standard to all of them and into the Board Standards. Close the resource-database gap by building Jewish-sourced content for the Learning Management Resources. This would also serve to aid our partners in crafting their own versions of how the Standards maps onto their work. The ultimate goal here is to build a movement of organizations in the nonprofit arena who are utilizing Jewish thought and practice, and wisdom as foundational pieces to their work.

Pillar 3 — Programs & Learning Experiences (Field Implementation)

Prioritize 2 existing cohort programs (Board Leadership Accelerator and Onboarding Intensive surfaced organically in early 1:1s as strong candidates) for a deeper Jewish-integration pilot, rather than spreading thin across all offerings at once. Design internal staff learning in parallel so Leading Edge models what it asks of the field, and so that staff begin to integrate Jewish learning independently. Further trainings will happen at the upcoming staff retreat as well as synchronous learning as this strategy is launched.

Pillar 4 — Thought Leadership & Field Voice

Use existing proof points (JPro27, the Weinberg Foundation's parallel Thriving Jewish Workplaces strategy, LKF, and the Maimonides partnership) as a platform to publish and speak on Jewish thought as sector infrastructure, building the external ecosystem named in the role's mandate in order to grow a movement.

5. Open Questions to Resolve

  • Where does the line sit between Leading Edge Torah as an internal grounding document and as an externally publishable artifact? Different audiences may need different versions.
  • Window dressing vs. driving force: how far does Leading Edge want to go in letting Jewish thought reshape the Standards and programs themselves, versus adding Jewish framing to what already exists? More about strategy and less about sequencing.
  • How should the role calibrate between depth (fewer, deeper Jewish learning experiences) and breadth (lighter-touch Jewish framing across everything)? 1:1s suggest the org has tried breadth-first and found it thins out the content.
  • What is Leading Edge's stance on resonating across the full range of Jewish identity and observance, and how explicitly should non-Jewish professionals be named as a co-equal audience for this work, given the recent survey findings?

These questions won't all resolve before the work starts. What follows is a first attempt at naming what success looks like regardless of how each one lands, so there's something concrete to test the answers against as they come in.

6. Measuring Success (Draft)

Leading Edge already has measurement infrastructure this work can plug into rather than reinvent. The Employee Experience Survey and CEO Survey could carry a Jewish-integration lens in future cycles, and concepts like heshbon hanefesh, the self-reckoning practice, along with other middot-adjacent frameworks map directly onto the 360 review and EES structure already in place, so this becomes a new lens on an existing one.

The forthcoming Snapshot tool is an opportunity to build Jewish-integration questions from the start rather than add them later, although given the timing, a later integration will need to happen. The LMS' percentage of Jewish-sourced material is a simple, trackable metric already flagged as a Phase 2 priority. And qualitatively, staff and program-participant reflections on whether Jewish content felt authentic and integrated, versus window dressing, are worth building into a simple feedback mechanism for any pilot.

Metrics tell us whether the work moved; they don't tell us whether it landed. Here's a first pass at what someone should see, hear, and feel walking away from Leading Edge Jewish learning programming:

LE Staff

Sees: A team meeting or retreat opens with a live text study tied directly to that week's actual decision, a hiring choice, a hard resource tradeoff, with the connection drawn out by the team itself.

Hears: A staff member reaching for heshbon hanefesh unprompted before giving feedback in a retreat debrief or an EES conversation, “let me do my own accounting first,” rather than a generic feedback-culture phrase.

Feels: A newer staff member, regardless of their Jewish background, experiences the team's Jewish framing as an invitation to think differently about the work, echoing back at a later point how it recalibrated their work flow.

Board

Sees: In a real, contested board decision, a budget reallocation, a nomination, junior board members are asked to weigh in before senior ones, mirroring the Sanhedrin's rule that junior judges vote first so conviction doesn't calcify around whoever's most senior.

Hears: A board member names a live disagreement as machloket l'shem shamayim, that the disagreement itself is doing legitimate work, rather than something to be smoothed over or resolved by Robert's Rules alone.

Feels: A first-year board member feels like their Jewish knowledge, or lack of it, has no bearing on whether their voice carries weight in the room; the practice is legible and usable regardless of where they started.

Partners

field programs, Standards-adopting organizations, JPro

Sees: A partner organization's leadership team uses the bein adam l'makom / chaveiro / atzmo framework introduced to them through the Onboarding Intensive, to structure their own strategic planning retreat, sorting agenda items into obligations to mission, to colleagues, and to self, with no LE staff in the room to translate it for them.

Hears: A manager at a partner organization names a hard conversation as tokhechah in plain language, private, specific, aimed at growth rather than protecting the record.

Feels: A new hire in a partner organization's Onboarding Intensive builds their own middot self-assessment in week one, experiencing it as an invitation into their own growth, whether they arrived fluent in the sources or with none.

7. A Framework for Durable Change

The Leading Edge Torah document lays out how Jewish tradition itself understands change — teshuvah's discipline of starting over, the back-and-forth rhythm of ratzo v'shov, the willingness to let a structure end so the mission survives it. This section turns from that grounding to the mechanics: how a new Jewish-learning practice actually spreads across an organization the size of Leading Edge, and across the field it serves. It draws on the same behavioral-change research Leading Edge already uses for field mobilization, applied here specifically to how Jewish thought and practice takes hold.

Five drivers determine whether a practice sticks or stays decorative:

  • Name and Codify the Behavior: Integration stays abstract until it's named as a specific, observable practice rather than a value statement — which Standard conversation, which recurring meeting, which decision point it actually attaches to.
  • Create a Visibility / Mirror Mechanism: A number people can see themselves against moves behavior more reliably than an aspirational statement — a Standards self-assessment, or the LMS's percentage of Jewish-sourced material.
  • Activate Early Adopters as Social Proof: The staff and programs already asking for this unprompted, flagged in early 1:1s, are the credible signal that moves the next cohort — not a mandate handed down from above.
  • Embed Accountability Structures: Teshuvah and heshbon hanefesh give this work a home inside instruments Leading Edge already runs — the EES, the 360 review cycle — rather than a new optional add-on.
  • Lower the Activation Energy: A short practice built into a meeting people already attend, a team retreat, a BLA session, beats a seminar they have to opt into. Belief follows the doing.

None of this asks for everyone converted at once. Mapping the field against Rogers' adoption curve clarifies what each segment actually needs to move:

What accelerates integration, and what stalls it, in practice:

PatternIn Practice
AcceleratesA named, observable practice tied to a real decision — a hire, a budget call — not an abstract value statement.
AcceleratesEarly, visible proof in a couple of flagship places (the BLA, the Onboarding Intensive) before asking for field-wide adoption.

The Missing Accelerant: The Accountability Loop. Metrics tell us whether the work moved; the accountability loop tells us whether it's expected to keep moving. Without one, early enthusiasm fades once the Innovators and Early Adopters have had their moment. The EES and CEO Survey, the 360 review cycle, and a Standards self-assessment are existing instruments that can carry that loop forward — a new lens on structures Leading Edge already runs, rather than a new one to maintain.
